SCM01 — Architecting Autonomous Supply Chain Strategy

SuccessLabs Academy · OmniVerse Energy Ltd. · Level 0 → Pre-Foundation
IIL — Industry Immersion Lab · Version 1.0 · 10 November 2026
Format: Origin Story Sprint · 120 minutes · Free — Community/Citizen Edition
Status: Published learning content; not an enrolled course or certification.

Your promise: In two hours, design one network-strategy decision that keeps service promises, costs and risk owners accountable.

One story · One build card · Three tabletop tests

1. Start with the story

A network model recommends consolidating to one depot to cut cost. The refinery manager warns that a planned turnaround would leave one route for critical supplies.

Supply chain lead: Wei Zhang — Chief Supply Chain & Procurement Officer (OVE-P11).
Counterpart: Hannah Fischer — Director of Enterprise Architecture (OVE-P12).
Operations or customer voice: Meera Iyer — Refinery Operations Manager, Mumbai (OVE-P14).

Wei Zhang asks what must be true before the team decides. Hannah Fischer asks which evidence and owner support the decision. Meera Iyer asks for the outcome that matters to the people affected.

Evidence reveal: In the synthetic network, the lane has no alternate route during planned maintenance, so the cost score hides a service dependency.

Pause: What service promise must stay true? Which constraint is missing from the model, and who can confirm it? Ask the counterpart one discovery question before choosing a solution.

Case boundary: OmniVerse Energy Ltd. is an aspirational case study (USD 85B revenue; 68,000 employees; 42 countries). These incidents, services and dialogue are proposed learning scenarios, not corporate history, confirmed deployments or achieved results. Preserve the enterprise persona IDs above. Country-specific permissions, contracts and operating rules require validation; this sprint does not invent them.

2. Trace the origin: 1970–2026

These overlapping periods are teaching lenses, not invention dates or a universal adoption sequence. Earlier practices can coexist with newer ones. Pre-1978 scenes describe the wider domain, not OmniVerse Energy Ltd. history.

  • 1970s–1980s: Distribution is planned by experience and paper; networks grow by habit.
  • 1980s–1990s: Planning systems and optimisation models appear; assumptions are often hidden in the model.
  • 2000s–2010s: Global sourcing and lean inventory cut cost; disruption exposes single points of failure.
  • 2020s–2026: Scenario tools and assisted analysis speed options; a named owner still decides which risk to carry.

Mindset shift: start with the service promise and who carries the risk, then choose the model. Optimising an unclear objective can move cost and risk to someone else.

Pause: Which old problem survives in a newer interface? Who bears its cost?

3. Explore possible futures: 2030–2050

2030 experiment: allow assistance to draft scenarios and surface constraints; retain the promise, a named decision owner and a challenge route.

2040 question: if partners share signals directly, can each node's constraints be visible without exposing commercial detail?

2050 scene: Imagine a network that rebalances itself within agreed promises. Providers may react faster, but people must own the promise and the trade-offs.

Competing future: A simpler network with buffers and clear fallback may beat a lean, adaptive one on resilience and trust.

All future passages are design hypotheses, not forecasts or product roadmaps. Test a useful part today rather than assume the future will arrive as described.

4. Draw the smallest useful architecture

Capability — OVE-SCM01-OS-CAP01: service-promise and network decisioning. A capability is an ability; it is not an application or a sequence of steps.

Value stages — OVE-SCM01-OS-VS01: Define the service promise → model options → decide → plan → execute → learn.

Objects to distinguish: service promise, network node, lane, capacity, constraint and scenario.

Proposed responsibility: Supply chain owns the network decision; operations owns capacity and constraints; finance owns the cost view; the customer-facing owner confirms the promise. SCM01 owns the strategy loop, not the planning system build.

Protected boundary: A network scenario score does not prove the service promise can be kept, or that cost savings are not simply shifted to a partner or customer.

Planning and logistics applications are optional demonstration candidates, chosen after requirements. No product is assumed installed, licensed or able to cover the whole network. This sprint focuses on application responsibility while referencing data, integration and security owners.

5. Build one Decision Card

Use synthetic case OVE-SCM01-OS-SYN001. Synthetic depot ENE-N300, lane ENE-L300, planned turnaround window, service promise to the refinery. Keep everything on one card or screen.

  1. Need: the outcome, affected people and one missing fact.
  2. Evidence: relevant object IDs, source, validity or effective time, data purpose and permitted access.
  3. Ownership: business decision owner, authoritative application and receiving owner.
  4. Gate: prerequisite, allowed next state, authorized actor and the message anyone affected will see.
  5. Alternative: Compare the lowest-cost network with an option that protects the service promise and names the risk owner.
  6. Exception: one failure state, escalation owner and a safe recovery or assisted route.
  7. Proof: the three results below, one measure and a stop, revise or proceed decision.

Requirement — OVE-SCM01-OS-REQ01: link one network-strategy hypothesis to an owned intervention and observed follow-up, including negative evidence.

Decision — OVE-SCM01-OS-ADR01: record your chosen option and the alternative from item 5, one trade-off, and the evidence that would change your choice.

6. Run three tabletop tests

T01 — The scenario assumes one route is always available

Expected: Add the maintenance window as a constraint and test an alternate route.

T02 — The cost saving shifts risk to a partner

Expected: Show who carries the risk and record the owner.

T03 — A disruption closes the only route

Expected: Trigger the fallback route and a named decision owner.

For each, record input → expected → actual paper result → evidence note → revision → retest. These are sprint-local tests OVE-SCM01-OS-T01/T02/T03, supporting OVE-SCM01-OS-REQ01. A completed lesson is not an executed tenant test.

Measure — OVE-SCM01-OS-KPI01: Service-promise attainment for the chosen segment, with a defined window and comparison population; record where risk is carried. Assign Wei Zhang as proposed measure owner, establish the baseline and exclusions, and review after the experiment. No numeric improvement is asserted.

Acceptance: the card identifies source and owner, preserves the protected boundary, handles all three tests after documented revisions and explains the decision in 60 seconds. Missing evidence remains visible. Record what is still unverified.

7. Use your 120 minutes

  • 0–20: read the origin timeline; explain what changed and what did not.
  • 20–45: role-play the story; ask the lead, counterpart and voice one useful question each.
  • 45–65: draw responsibilities and consult one resource selectively.
  • 65–100: complete the single Decision Card and compare the alternative.
  • 100–115: run the three tests; revise from observed paper behavior.
  • 115–120: explain the decision in 60 seconds and choose the next experiment.

All reading fits within these blocks. Do not complete an entire product course during the sprint.

Whose risk did your option move? Which constraint was missing? What evidence would make you reject your design?
