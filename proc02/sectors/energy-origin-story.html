1. The purchase behind the process

Three suppliers bid for a refinery maintenance component. One quotes the lowest price but omits a mandatory technical requirement; another includes delivery and commissioning that the others exclude.

Lead: Wei Zhang — Chief Supply Chain & Procurement Officer (OVE-P11).
Counterpart: Carlos Mendes — VP Asset Reliability and Maintenance (OVE-P09).

Wei asks whether the bids are comparable. Carlos asks which criteria are mandatory and who can validate technical evidence before commercial ranking.

The team publishes the evaluation basis before judging results, normalizes scope and sends missing evidence to the owner. A weighted score cannot compensate for an unmet mandatory gate.

Pause: What business need and evidence must be clear before the next commitment? Ask both people one useful question.

OmniVerse is a aspirational integrated energy group with 68,000 employees in 42 countries. Existing OVE-P personas retain their source identities. Dialogue, incidents, supplier records and proposed outcomes here are new learning fiction. No real supplier, award, transaction or implementation result is represented.

2. Origins and evolution, 1970–2025

These closed, non-overlapping periods are teaching lenses, not invention dates or universal adoption deadlines. Practices coexist. Pre-1978 examples describe the wider domain rather than OmniVerse corporate history.

  • 1970–1989: Paper requests and negotiations depend on local records.
  • 1990–1999: Bid spreadsheets compare offers but can mix scope and units.
  • 2000–2009: Electronic sourcing organizes questions and approvals.
  • 2010–2019: Structured events and supplier collaboration improve traceability.
  • 2020–2025: Assisted evaluation summarizes bids; evidence quality and impartial criteria still govern awards.

Mindset: optimize the business outcome and relationship, with evidence for each consequential decision. A faster purchase can still create a larger downstream problem.

Social impact question: whose safety, livelihood, access to opportunity or environment could this choice affect?

3. AI-driven horizon, 2026–2050

2026 experiment: use assistance for a narrow evidence summary, draft or exception suggestion. Test what it misses before delegating any action.

2030 checkpoint: compare assisted work with a human-led baseline, including contrary evidence and a challenge route.

2035–2050 scenario: Imagine a 2050 sourcing assistant collecting verifiable offers. It may prepare comparisons but must preserve confidential bids, approved criteria and a reviewable award rationale.

Competing future: Where suppliers have limited digital access, assisted participation must preserve comparable requirements and equal information.

These are design hypotheses, not forecasts, legal interpretations or vendor roadmaps. Autonomy remains bounded by approved authority.

4. Smallest useful architecture

Capability — OVE-PROC02-OS-CAP01: supplier sourcing.

Value stages — OVE-PROC02-OS-VS01: Define need → approve criteria → invite → clarify → evaluate → recommend → approve award.

Objects to distinguish: sourcing request, requirement, bid version, scope adjustment, criterion, gate and award recommendation.

Application responsibility: Sourcing owns event/criteria; engineering owns technical gates; Finance reviews commercial comparisons; authorized business owners approve awards. Contract commitment follows PROC03.

Protected boundary: A ranking is a recommendation. It does not qualify unsafe equipment or execute a contract.

Start with the business need before choosing SAP Ariba, SAP Business Network, ERP or another platform. Product scope, editions, local obligations and licensing require validation. The twelve PROC themes and twelve architecture streams are separate axes.

5. One Decision Card

Use synthetic case OVE-PROC02-OS-SYN001. The Google workbook supplies editable fields; the deck supports discussion. Together they decode this same sprint rather than adding separate required deliverables.

  1. Need: one decision, affected population and missing fact.
  2. Evidence: source/business IDs, specification or scope, validity, unit/currency where relevant.
  3. Owner: accountable business decision maker, source application and receiving owner.
  4. Gate: prerequisite, allowed next state and exception condition.
  5. Alternative: Compare price-only ranking with gated technical qualification followed by an approved commercial comparison.
  6. Recovery: escalation owner and safe correction/assisted route.
  7. Proof: three actual paper results, a measure and stop/revise/proceed decision.

Requirement — OVE-PROC02-OS-REQ01: retain comparable bid evidence, mandatory gates and authorized award rationale.

Decision — OVE-PROC02-OS-ADR01: record the chosen option, alternative, trade-off and evidence that would change your choice.

6. Three tabletop tests

T01 — Lowest bidder lacks mandatory evidence

Expected: Hold its applicable gate and request validation before award recommendation.

T02 — Criterion changes after bids are visible

Expected: Control and disclose the change; reassess fairness and required re-evaluation.

T03 — Bid price excludes installation

Expected: Normalize approved scope assumptions and show the adjustment separately.

Record input → expected → actual paper result → evidence → reviewer decision → revision/retest. Sprint-local tests OVE-PROC02-OS-T01/T02/T03 support REQ01. Publishing expected results does not prove execution.

Measure — OVE-PROC02-OS-KPI01: award recommendations with complete criteria/gate/comparison evidence / recommendations reviewed. Confirm the owner, baseline, period and exclusions before setting targets.

Acceptance: the card names owners and sources, preserves the protected boundary, handles all three tests after documented revisions and explains the decision in 60 seconds. Keep unverified facts visible.

7. Your 120 minutes

  • 0–20: read the evolution; explain the problem that persists.
  • 20–45: role-play the lead and counterpart; request evidence.
  • 45–65: map application responsibilities and inspect one resource.
  • 65–100: complete the card and compare the alternative.
  • 100–115: run the three tests and revise from observed paper behavior.
  • 115–120: explain your decision and choose the next experiment.
