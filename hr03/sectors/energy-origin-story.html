When high output is not high performance

A two-hour Origin Story sprint: Architecting High-Performance Organizations

SuccessLabs Academy · HR03-APH3 · Energy · Level 0 → Pre-Foundation
Platform: SAP · Domain: Human Capital Management · Architecture: Applied Application Architecture (AA)

Essential idea: Performance management evolved from judging yesterday's employee to enabling tomorrow's contribution. In energy, output alone cannot define success: how safely, responsibly and sustainably results are achieved matters.

1. What this sprint achieves

In 120 minutes, you will: 1. Explain performance management's general purpose and its evolution over 1975–2026. 2. Compare possible futures through 2050 without treating scenarios as predictions. 3. Distinguish goals, measures, evidence, feedback, ratings, calibration and development. 4. Map application responsibility across performance, operating evidence, workforce facts, learning and rewards. 5. Build and test two role scorecards and a review/appeal design using synthetic evidence.

Output: one Performance Evolution Build Card: timeline, two role scorecards, application ownership, a short architecture decision and three tabletop tests.

The 20% to master: balanced contribution; role/context relevance; trustworthy evidence; useful coaching; accountable review and appeal.

Practical outcome: implementation preparation and a paper prototype, not production SAP configuration proficiency in two hours.

2. Performance management in general — origins and purpose

Organizations have long evaluated work to allocate responsibility, pay, promotion and development. Formal appraisal made these judgments more repeatable, but also concentrated attention on periodic ratings. Management by objectives, scorecards, feedback and continuous conversations brought different answers to a persistent question: how can people contribute to organizational outcomes while improving their ability to do the work?

Performance appraisal is an evaluation activity. Performance management is the wider cycle of agreeing expectations, enabling work, reviewing evidence, discussing contribution and acting on development needs. A high-performance organization also depends on work design, leadership, resources, team coordination and trust. Installing a review application cannot create all of these.

Purpose: agree what valuable contribution means, support it during the period, evaluate it fairly and decide what to improve next.

Boundary: a performance rating is not a salary calculation, verified safety authorization, medical judgment or measure of a person's worth.

Five generations as a mental model

Judge → align → digitize → coach → assist. These are overlapping teaching lenses, not an official maturity model. Annual reviews can coexist with effective continuous coaching. More frequent feedback is not automatically better feedback.

Evolution over the last 50 years

Dates below describe broad patterns, not invention dates or universal adoption. Earlier practices remain in use.

Era How the process worked / changed What improved Unresolved problem Illustrative energy translation
1975–1984: annual appraisal and objectives Managers record annual judgments, discuss results and sometimes link objectives to pay or promotion Explicit expectations and a documented review moment Recency effects, subjective judgments and limited employee voice Refinery output targets can dominate if safe work and quality are not explicit
1985–1994: competencies and broader scorecards Organizations increasingly combine outcomes with capabilities/behaviours and multiple business dimensions A wider definition of contribution Generic competency language and too many measures can obscure actual work Recognize collaboration and prevention alongside throughput
1995–2004: electronic appraisal and multi-source feedback Forms move into HR systems; some organizations expand 360-degree feedback and goal cascades Easier administration and broader perspectives Digitized forms can reproduce weak criteria; feedback may be mistaken for objective fact Engineers receive peer input, but a survey cannot replace validated operating evidence
2005–2014: connected talent processes Performance records increasingly inform learning, succession and rewards in integrated suites Reuse and visible development/reward connections One review may be overloaded with incompatible purposes; downstream effects amplify poor evidence A questionable rating can influence both a development opportunity and a bonus
2015–2019: stronger emphasis on continuous conversations Some employers supplement/redesign annual appraisals with check-ins, feedback and shorter goal cycles Earlier course correction and more opportunity for coaching Check-in counts can reward ritual rather than useful support; structured review still has a role Discuss changing project priorities before year-end, with an auditable goal revision
2020–2022: distributed and disrupted work Remote/hybrid work where feasible increases focus on outcomes, manager support and changing circumstances Greater attention to work context and enabling conditions Visibility bias and inappropriate comparisons; plant work cannot simply become remote Compare a field technician's contribution with weather/access constraints, not office presence
2023–2026: assistance and evidence quality Products increasingly offer AI drafting, summaries and conversation support; organizations examine skills and team contribution Potentially less administration and better-prepared conversations Invented summaries, hidden surveillance, biased inputs and unsupported ratings A fluent draft must not turn disputed HSE facts into an approved “top performer” narrative

Evidence anchor: CIPD's performance-management resources describe movement toward regular supportive conversations while retaining a role for structured reviews. Its evidence review cautions against assuming fashionable process changes necessarily improve results. The dated table above is a broad educational synthesis, not a universal empirical chronology. [R1–R2]

Current product anchor: SAP documentation covers goals, continuous coaching/feedback, reviews, calibration and 360 reviews; SAP's 2H 2025 announcement describes assistance for preparing performance conversations. This does not prove that a particular tenant has licensed/enabled those features or that an assistant should decide ratings. [R3–R4]

All future rows are illustrative scenarios, not SAP roadmaps, guaranteed outcomes or forecasts of employment practice.

Horizon Possible process Energy application Design obligation
2030: assisted preparation Approved goals + permitted evidence → assisted draft → employee/manager discussion → reviewed decision Maya prepares conversations across refinery and wind-service teams Cite evidence, show uncertainty, allow correction; humans own decisions

2040: learning and performance loops Agree challenge → test improvement → gather evidence → reflect → revise work and skills Teams learn from maintenance and transition-project experiments Separate experimentation/learning from punitive surveillance; retain approved review policy

Alternative 2050 world Risk Useful choice now
Trusted coaching ecosystem — assistance supports reflection and fair contribution People without rich digital records may be overlooked Accept multiple evidence types and employee corrections

What may endure from 1975 to 2050: relevant expectations, fair evidence, a useful conversation, accountable judgment and a next development step.

Future experiment: would a draft assistant still be useful if ratings were removed? If so, identify the coaching outcome; if not, question whether the application is optimizing a form rather than contribution.

5. The story — Maya asks what “excellent” means

All dialogue is newly written fiction using source personas. Historical scenes are teaching comparisons, not their biographies.

Scene 1 — The annual envelope

Maya shows an imagined 1970s paper appraisal. Victor says the manager knows who delivered. Amara asks where the form records the person who prevented a failure that never appeared in output figures.

Lesson: a rating form makes judgment visible; it does not guarantee a balanced definition of success.
Pause: What would be invisible if output were the only criterion?

Scene 2 — The digital copy

The workshop compares a paper form with its electronic equivalent. Both contain the same vague instruction: “show initiative.”

Maya asks for an observable example and relevant evidence. The team sees that digitization changes administration before it changes judgment.

Lesson: define meaning and evidence before configuring fields.
Pause: Rewrite one vague criterion into a role-relevant expectation.

Scene 3 — Unsafe success

Victor recommends top ratings because throughput is high. Amara presents rising near misses and emissions deviations. Fatima asks whether facts are validated, how policy treats them and whether affected employees can respond.

Maya does not mechanically equate reported near misses with poor individual performance: more reporting can reflect better safety culture. She asks HSE to distinguish reporting activity, exposure and substantiated behaviour before making a decision.

Lesson: do not reward unsafe success or punish honest reporting.
Pause: What must be checked before any rating changes?

Scene 4 — One form, different work

Jin's service tasks are delayed by weather restrictions. A corporate form compares his completion count with work performed under very different conditions.

Maya keeps shared principles but asks for role-specific measures and approved context. She does not retrospectively lower targets merely to produce a preferred rating.

Lesson: comparability requires relevant context, not identical targets.
Pause: Which factor is within Jin's influence, and what reliable record proves the external constraint?

Scene 5 — The rating without a conversation

Jin receives a rating but no actionable development plan. Fatima finds unexplained differences across managers.

Maya proposes an evidence-led calibration conversation, an appeal route and one agreed development action with an owner and follow-up date.

Lesson: calibration examines evidence and consistency; it is not an automatic forced distribution.
Pause: What would make the review useful even if no rating changed?

Scene 6 — An imagined 2050 review

In a future design workshop, Maya asks the group to imagine a team working across storage, wind and conventional assets. An assistant summarizes contribution, but attributes a team outage to one worker and omits a corrected maintenance record.

The imagined worker challenges the draft. The team retrieves sources, corrects the summary and records the authorized decision. The assistant saves preparation time only if the evidence remains inspectable.

Lesson: more advanced assistance increases the importance of provenance and challenge.
Pause: What can you prototype today to test this 2050 idea?

6. Concepts and application ownership

Concept Plain meaning Mistake to avoid
Goal Agreed contribution or outcome Treating a list of activities as the outcome
Measure / target How progress is assessed / intended level Ignoring denominator, period, context or quality
Evidence Information supporting a judgment Treating an assertion or AI summary as a validated operating fact
Feedback / coaching Observations and support for improvement Equating message frequency with quality
Review / rating Structured evaluation / coded conclusion under policy Assuming the score tells the complete story
Calibration Authorized comparison of evidence and interpretation Forcing a curve or negotiating scores without reasons
Appeal A governed way to challenge a decision Treating finalization as removal of employee voice
Development action Agreed learning/practice/support step Giving a rating without enabling improvement
Application / accountable area Owns or provides Does not decide alone
Performance & Goals / Continuous Performance Management Goals, review workflow and permitted performance evidence, feedback and decision records Validity of every imported operating fact
HSE / environmental / quality systems Validated operating facts within their domain and investigation status Individual performance ratings
Asset/work management Maintenance/work-order and asset context Causal attribution of all team results to one person
Employee Central — HR01 Approved employment, manager and organizational context Performance contribution or final pay outcomes
Learning / development — HR06 and HR04 Learning evidence and agreed development pathways within scope A guaranteed rating improvement or promotion
Rewards — HR05 Compensation policy/planning consuming approved results Raw manager assertions as automatically approved pay decisions
Analytics/AI — HR09/HR11; integration — HR12 Governed measures/assistance and controlled exchanges Replacement of source owners or accountable reviewers

8. Build experiment — high throughput, missing context

Synthetic examples: review RV-001 for a refinery team member; RV-002 for a wind-service technician. These are teaching records, not named employees' actual results. A refinery reports strong throughput with disputed safety/environmental evidence. The wind-service review shows missed work during a documented weather restriction.

Draft two role scorecards

No weights or automatic rating formulas are prescribed. Policy owners must decide aggregation and how substantiated critical breaches affect review/reward eligibility. A favourable average must not conceal a substantiated critical issue.

Dimension Refinery example Wind-service example Evidence owner
Outcome Reliable production meeting approved quality requirements Service contribution to reliable asset operation Operations / asset owner
Safe and responsible work Compliance with approved work practices and transparent reporting Safe work within access/weather limits HSE / authorized operations
Quality/environment Relevant validated quality and emissions measures Quality of repairs; applicable environmental practices Quality / environmental owner
Team contribution Handover quality and constructive improvement Crew collaboration and travel/coordination contribution Relevant manager/peers, with evidence
Development Agreed practice to address a role-relevant gap Relevant competence/development action Employee/manager; learning evidence where relevant

Avoid a harmful metric: “zero reported near misses” can discourage reporting. Separate reporting culture from substantiated unsafe behaviour and exposure. Likewise, do not use continuous activity monitoring as a proxy for contribution.

Three tabletop tests

Test Challenge Expected result in the proposed design
T1 — Unsafe success / disputed evidence High output; safety/environmental concerns not yet resolved No automatic top rating or simplistic penalty; validate domain facts and apply approved policy; unresolved critical evidence remains visible with an owner
T2 — Context and late goal change Weather restriction affects results; manager changes goal retrospectively Preserve agreed goal/version; validate context; require justified authorized revision, employee visibility and review of comparability
T3 — Contested AI draft / downstream result Draft invents a negative claim; employee appeals; rewards asks for results Correct from sources; human decision/appeal route applies; only approved results release under explicit policy, with disputes/status visible and no silent overwriting

Actual result means your paper result. Record if the initial design failed and what changed. No executed tenant tests or operational success is claimed.

Acceptance: explain the historical evolution and a competing 2050 scenario; distinguish key concepts; map source ownership; satisfy all three tabletop expectations after revision; give Jin a useful development action. A safety-reporting penalty, fabricated evidence or unreviewed consequential decision requires redesign.

Measure: OVE-HR03-KPI01 = reviews with agreed evidence and check-ins / reviews due. Define “agreed evidence,” check-in quality, denominator/exclusions and as-of date. Pair coverage with appeal outcomes and role fairness. Review completion alone does not prove better performance; correlation with operating results is not proof of causation.
