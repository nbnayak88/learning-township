# They stayed calm in the meeting and carried it home
## LIT03 — Architecting Emotional Freedom

**IIL — Industry Immersion Lab · Version 1.0 · 10 November 2026**

**One story · One practice card · Three real-life tests**

## 1. Start with the story

After a difficult meeting in a media and networks group with a constant news cycle, David Chen keeps their composure all day and then snaps at family in the evening.

**The person facing the choice:** David Chen — Group CFO (OCN-P02).  
**A trusted colleague:** Amelia Jones — Subscriber and Enterprise Client Representative (OCN-P15).  
**Someone affected:** Mei Lin — Subscriber Data and Consent Lead (OCN-P09).

David Chen asks what matters most before choosing. Amelia Jones asks what evidence and which support would help. Mei Lin asks how the choice will feel to the people around them.

**What they notice:** A short noticing practice shows the tension began in the meeting but was never named, so it surfaced later.

**Pause:** What would you want to be true before you decide? Ask the colleague one curious question before choosing.

**Case boundary:** OmniConnect Media Networks Ltd. is an aspirational case study (USD 37B communications group; 62,000 employees; 28 countries; 210M subscriptions). This story, its people and its dialogue are proposed learning scenarios, not corporate history, real individuals or achieved results. Preserve the persona IDs above. Reflection is voluntary and personal; share only what you choose to.

## 2. Trace the origin: 1970–2026

These overlapping periods are teaching lenses, not invention dates or a universal sequence. Earlier practices can coexist with newer ones.

- **1970s–1980s:** Emotions are seen as private; composure is rewarded at work.
- **1980s–1990s:** Stress management enters workplaces; talking about feelings is still unusual.
- **2000s–2010s:** Emotional intelligence and mindfulness spread; they are sometimes oversold.
- **2020s–2026:** Wellbeing apps and prompts are common; people still need a trusted human when it matters.

**Mindset shift:** Name the feeling first; choosing a response comes after.

**Pause:** Which old habit survives in a newer tool? Who bears its cost?

## 3. Explore possible futures: 2030–2050

**2030 experiment:** Try a two-minute end-of-day check-in and share it only if you wish.

**2040 question:** Ask how workplaces can make honest conversation safe without asking anyone to disclose more than they want.

**2050 scene:** Imagine communities where naming a feeling is ordinary and where professional help is easy to reach.

**Competing view:** Some people process feelings best through action or conversation rather than writing; a different route may suit them better.

All future passages are design hypotheses, not forecasts. Test a small useful part today rather than assume the future will arrive as described.

## 4. Draw the smallest useful map

**Practice — OCN-LIT03-OS-CAP01:** emotional-awareness practice. A practice is something you do repeatedly; it is not a one-time insight.

**Steps — OCN-LIT03-OS-VS01:** Notice → name → pause → choose a response → repair if needed → reflect.

**Things to keep distinct:** trigger, feeling, body signal, response and repair.

**Five life areas:** spiritual, social, academic, professional and personal. Note which areas this practice touches and which it leaves alone.

**Protected boundary:** This practice is personal reflection, not therapy, counselling or a way to diagnose anything.

## 5. Build one Practice Card

Use synthetic case **OCN-LIT03-OS-SYN001**. Synthetic person COM-LP300, meeting COM-MT300, evening notes COM-NT300. Keep everything on one card.

1. **Need:** the situation, who is affected and one missing fact.
2. **Values:** two or three values at stake, in your own words.
3. **Commitment:** one small action you can keep within a week, and who will know.
4. **Evidence:** what you will notice, record or ask to see whether it helps.
5. **Alternative:** Compare holding the feeling in with naming it and choosing a response.
6. **When to get support:** one sign that means you should talk to a trusted person or a qualified professional. 
7. **Review:** the date you will look again and what would make you stop or change course.

**Requirement — OCN-LIT03-OS-REQ01:** link one emotional-awareness practice hypothesis to a small action and an observed follow-up, including evidence that it did not help.

**Decision — OCN-LIT03-OS-ADR01:** record your chosen option and the alternative from item 5, one trade-off, and what would change your mind.

## 6. Run three real-life tests

### T01 — A strong emotion arrives in a meeting

**Expected response:** Pause, name it privately and choose a response.

### T02 — The feeling follows them home

**Expected response:** Acknowledge it and give the people at home a plain, kind reason.

### T03 — The feeling does not ease over time

**Expected response:** Reach out to a trusted person or a qualified professional; do not carry it alone.

For each, record **situation → what you would do → what you notice → revision → retry**. These are sprint-local tests OCN-LIT03-OS-T01/T02/T03, supporting OCN-LIT03-OS-REQ01. Completing the lesson is not a measured change in your life.

**Signal — OCN-LIT03-OS-KPI01:** Notice how often a feeling was named before a response, noted privately; no outcome is promised. Choose your own baseline and review date. No improvement is promised.

**Acceptance:** the card names your values, keeps the boundary above, handles all three tests after revision and can be explained in 60 seconds. Anything unknown stays visible.

## 7. Use your 120 minutes

- **0–20:** read the origin timeline; explain what changed and what did not.
- **20–45:** role-play the story; ask the person, colleague and affected person one useful question each.
- **45–65:** map your own situation across the five life areas.
- **65–100:** complete the single Practice Card and compare the alternative.
- **100–115:** run the three tests and revise.
- **115–120:** explain your choice in 60 seconds and choose a first small action.

## 9. Reflect and continue

What did you notice? Who could you talk to if it grew heavier? If this brings up strong feelings, pause and talk to someone you trust or a qualified professional.
